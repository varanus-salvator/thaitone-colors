Colors

A searchable digitisation of the physical ThaiTone™ color fan deck — 330 Thai traditional colors. Each color shows its code, Thai name, romanisation, English meaning, the CMYK as printed on the deck, and an sRGB hex derived by color-managed conversion for on-screen preview.

How these colors work

CMYK is the source; the hex is an interpretation. The CMYK values are printed on the physical cards and are exact. There is no single “correct” RGB for a CMYK recipe — it depends on the assumed print profile and rendering intent — so the sRGB hex here is one color-managed conversion, chosen as a safe, device-independent reference.

Why a screen can’t match print. Screens are additive (emitted light); ink is subtractive (reflected light), and their gamuts only partly overlap. Saturated reds and oranges that ink prints well get clipped by sRGB, so these reference swatches are the quietest version of the colors.

Thai light brings them to life. ThaiTone™ colors live outdoors in bright tropical sun, under cool mall lighting, in warm golden hour, and in the flat grey of the rainy season. Perceived colorfulness rises with illumination (the Hunt effect) and shifts with the light’s color temperature, so the same color reads very differently across these settings. The viewing-light disc on the colors page (the Light button) is an illustrative approximation of that: drag the point (vertical = brightness, horizontal = warm↔cool color temperature) and the swatches recolor live. Named Thai scenes — beach, city, golden hour, food, night, rainy season — are anchor points you can jump to. It applies a documented transform (saturation and lightness from the Hunt effect, plus a white-point tint) and changes only the preview swatch and the hex shown beside it — the underlying CMYK and the sRGB reference never change.

Why color is a wheel, not a line. Physics lays color on a straight line by wavelength (red…violet). Artists bend it into a wheel — and the reason is that purple does not exist as a wavelength: it’s a sensation the brain invents when the short- and long-wave cones fire together, joining the two ends of the line. That is why ThaiTone’s own sequence runs red → … → red-violet and closes back toward red. The Tint→shade order on the colors page arranges each hue family by the painter’s second axis — tint (with white) → tone (greyed) → shade (with black). A lovely explanation on video ↗

Some colors a screen simply cannot show. The brightest inks — fluorescent ones especially — can throw back more visible light than a display can emit, so they read as dull earth tones on any screen while being vivid in the hand. It’s the same reason the printed deck, not this page, is the real reference.
