How these colors work

CMYK is the source; the hex is an interpretation

INKSCREEN

The CMYK on the cards is exact. There is no single “correct” RGB for a CMYK recipe — it depends on the print profile and rendering intent — so our hex is one color-managed conversion, a safe, device-independent reference.

Screens emit light, ink reflects it, and their gamuts only partly overlap. Saturated reds and oranges that print well get clipped on screen, so these swatches are the quietest version of the colors. The brightest inks, fluorescents especially, can even look like dull earth tones on any display while being vivid in the hand — the printed deck stays the real reference.

The same color — Dangchad, vermilion, T1010 — reads very differently in blazing beach sun, warm golden hour, the flat grey of the rainy season or a city night. Colorfulness rises with brightness (the Hunt effect) and shifts with the light’s color temperature.

The viewing-light disc on the colors page lets you try it: drag the point (up = brighter, left/right = warm/cool) or tap a Thai scene, and every swatch and hex recolors live. It is an illustrative approximation; the CMYK and the sRGB reference never change.

Why color is a wheel, not a line

→

Physics lays color on a straight line by wavelength, red to violet. Artists bend it into a wheel, because purple does not exist as a wavelength: the brain invents it when the short- and long-wave cones fire together, joining the two ends. That is why ThaiTone’s own sequence runs red → … → red-violet and closes back toward red. The Tint→shade order on the colors page sorts each family along the painter’s second axis: tint (with white), tone (greyed), shade (with black). A lovely explanation on video ↗
