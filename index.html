ThaiTone Color System

A searchable digitisation of the physical ThaiTone color fan deck — 330 Thai traditional colors. Each color shows its code, Thai name, romanisation, English meaning, the CMYK as printed on the deck, and an sRGB hex derived by color-managed conversion for on-screen preview.

Source color system & names: Prof. Pairoj Pittayamatee (ไพโรจน์ พิทยเมธี), creator of ThaiTone. Official announcement of the 330-color deck: ThaiTone Color (Facebook).
CMYK values are transcribed from the physical cards; hex is an independent conversion (not official). Provided for study, with attribution. Not affiliated with or endorsed by the author.
About these colors & the viewing-environment preview

CMYK is the source; the hex is an interpretation. The CMYK values are printed on the physical cards and are exact. There is no single “correct” RGB for a CMYK recipe — it depends on the assumed print profile and rendering intent — so the sRGB hex here is one color-managed conversion, chosen as a safe, device-independent reference.

Why a screen can’t match print. Screens are additive (emitted light); ink is subtractive (reflected light), and their gamuts only partly overlap. Saturated reds and oranges that ink prints well get clipped by sRGB, so these reference swatches are the quietest version of the colors.

Thai light brings them to life. ThaiTone colors live outdoors in bright tropical sun, under cool mall lighting, in warm golden hour, and in the flat grey of the rainy season. Perceived colorfulness rises with illumination (the Hunt effect) and shifts with the light’s color temperature, so the same color reads very differently across these settings. The viewing-environment disc below is an illustrative approximation of that: drag the point (vertical = brightness, horizontal = warm↔cool color temperature) and the swatches recolor live. Named Thai scenes — beach, city, golden hour, food, night, rainy season — are anchor points you can jump to. It applies a documented transform (saturation and lightness from the Hunt effect, plus a white-point tint) and changes only the preview swatch and the hex shown beside it — the underlying CMYK and the sRGB reference never change.
